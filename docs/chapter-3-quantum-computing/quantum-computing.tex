\section{Quantum Computing}
\label{sec:quantum-computing}

This chapter introduces the quantum-specific features of QLang: the built-in
quantum types, the primitive quantum gates available in the language, how
measurement works, and two canonical example protocols: quantum
teleportation and superdense coding.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Quantum Types: \texttt{state} and \texttt{obs}}
\label{sec:quantum-types}

QLang exposes two first-class types for quantum programming:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{state}: a quantum register (one or more qubits). A
        \texttt{state} can be put into superposition and entangled with other
        \texttt{state}s using the built-in gate primitives. A variable of type
        \texttt{state} represents a live quantum system that only yields a
        classical value when explicitly measured. Quantum states cannot be
        read, assigned, or printed directly.
    \item \texttt{obs}: a classical observation. A scalar \texttt{obs} stores
        a boolean value; a sized register \texttt{obs x[N]} stores an N-bit
        integer. Used for branching, control, and classical post-processing
        after measurement.
\end{itemize}

A variable declaration can specify arrays and bit-widths.
\texttt{state q[2]} declares a two-qubit register;
\texttt{obs result[8]} declares an 8-bit classical register.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Primitive Gates}
\label{sec:quantum-gates}

QLang provides primitive quantum gates as first-class language constructs.
Each gate has a short uppercase name and a lowercase alias.
The supported gates are listed below.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|p{9cm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Gate} & \textbf{Alias} & \textbf{Description} \\
\hline
\texttt{H}    & \texttt{superpose}       & Hadamard gate, creates superposition on a single qubit. \\
\texttt{S}    & \texttt{shift}           & Phase gate, introduces a phase shift on a single qubit. \\
\texttt{X}    & \texttt{not}             & Pauli-X, flips the computational basis state. \\
\texttt{Y}    & \texttt{dual\_not}       & Pauli-Y, combines a bit flip and a phase flip. \\
\texttt{Z}    & \texttt{phase\_not}      & Pauli-Z, applies a relative phase flip between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. \\
\texttt{CNOT} & \texttt{entangle}        & Controlled-NOT, entangles two qubits: syntax \texttt{CNOT a -> b}. \\
\texttt{CZ}   & \texttt{entangle\_phase} & Controlled-Z, applies a phase flip conditioned on the control qubit. \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Single-qubit gates act on one \texttt{state} variable in-place.
Multi-qubit gates use the arrow syntax (\texttt{->}) to indicate the
control-to-target relationship.

\subsection{Measurement}
\label{sec:measurement}

Measurement forces a \texttt{state} to collapse into a classical outcome.
The \texttt{measure} keyword returns a boolean value (\texttt{false} for
$|0\rangle$, \texttt{true} for $|1\rangle$), which is automatically mapped
to integer \texttt{0} or \texttt{1} when assigned to an \texttt{obs}
variable. Measuring multiple qubits at once produces an \texttt{obs} array.

QLang also provides \texttt{measureX}, which applies a Hadamard gate before
measuring, effectively measuring in the X basis rather than the Z basis.

\subsection{Example: Quantum Teleportation}
\label{sec:teleportation}

Quantum teleportation transfers the state of a qubit from one place to
another using a shared entangled pair and two classical bits of
communication. The sender measures their qubits and sends the classical
results; the receiver applies correction gates based on those results to
recover the original state.

\subsection{Example: Superdense Coding}
\label{sec:superdense}

Superdense coding allows two classical bits of information to be
transmitted using only one qubit, provided the sender and receiver share
an entangled pair beforehand. The sender encodes the two bits by applying
gates to their half of the pair and sends the qubit; the receiver decodes
both bits by measuring the pair jointly.
